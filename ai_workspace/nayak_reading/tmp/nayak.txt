Abstract
The growing dependence on weather-driven variable renewable energy (VRE) sources introduces
significant challenges for long-term energy system planning due to intra and inter-annual vari-
ability. Conventional approaches often rely on a single representative weather year, which can
bias estimates of resource availability, demand, and optimal investments. This study introduces
a framework that integrates scaled weather-dependent time series data into capacity expansion
planning, where the scaling preserves the long-term distributions of multi-decadal weather vari-
ability while remaining computationally tractable. The approach is embedded in a two-stage
modelling framework, where baseline investments are first optimized using the scaled time se-
ries, and system adequacy is subsequently evaluated across 39 historical weather years, with
additional investments allowed under extreme weather years to ensure adequacy. The frame-
work is applied to a pan-European, sector-coupled energy system for 2050. Results show that
single weather-year based planning introduces substantial biases in weather-dependent resource
availability and demand, which are mitigated through the proposed scaling approach. However,
incorporating long-term distributions alone is not sufficient to ensure adequacy, as the result-
ing baseline system remains vulnerable under extreme weather conditions. While investment
strategies based solely on extreme weather years can address adequacy concerns, the proposed
two-stage approach achieves comparable adequacy by reinforcing the baseline system with tar-
geted additional investments. We claim that VRE investment frameworks based on multi-decadal
weather data better reflect developer decision-making than approaches relying on single weather
years. These methodological differences lead to divergent capacity portfolios, including vary-
ing levels of dependence on storage and transmission infrastructure. Our results emphasize the
importance of jointly accounting for long-term weather conditions and extreme events in energy
system planning.

1. Introduction

1.1. Background and Literature review
     Rising emissions and related climate change impacts have led countries to pursue and plan
for National and regional de-carbonization pathways (e.g., EU Green Deal, Fit-for-55). A sig-
nificant body of literature has shown that transition towards carbon neutral energy systems is
possible with variable renewable energy (VRE) generation becoming the primary energy source,
mostly consisting of wind, solar and hydro power supported by flexibility across supply (stor-
age, hydro scheduling, peakers), demand (Demand Response, Electric vehicle (EV) smart charg-
ing), and transmission inter-connectors between regions in order to manage the intermittency
of VRE sources. Moreover, Sector coupling (heat, transport, industry) further enables deep de-
carbonization of the energy system by electrification of other sectors [1], [2], [3], [4].
     As VRE dominates the supply side and electrified heating and/or cooling reshape demand,
daily, seasonal and inter-annual weather variability becomes the principal driver of flexibility
requirements and costs [5], [6], [7]. These systems could potentially also face weather related
resource adequacy risks that are often low probability events (for example energy droughts or
dunkelflaute) and might lead to loss of load. These are events where energy from VRE sources
is lower than usual combined with higher than usual demand. To ensure robustness of future
weather dependent energy systems, it is necessary to ensure that these events are represented
well enough from energy system perspectives (e.g. the intensity, duration, frequency, sequence,
spatial distribution, and seasonality of such events) [8], [9], [10]. [11] reviewed large body
of literature that has analyzed these prolonged low-VRE periods across hydrology, meteorology,
wind and solar research, and energy-system modeling. They argue that there is a growing interest
in understanding periods of VRE shortage; however, studies lack a unifying terminology and
consistent methods; the authors therefore recommend harmonized definitions and clearly defined
parameters when reporting results to enable robust, comparable analyses.
     More recently, several studies have used multiple years of weather data to study extreme
system level events and design robust energy systems with energy system models. [12] used 40
years of weather data on Pan-European power system model to identify weather induced weather
events via resulting dual variables of energy balance and storage dynamic equations. They iden-
tify stressful events being driven by large-scale weather conditions such as low wind and cold
temperature periods of various lengths associated with stationary high pressure over Europe.
They show that purely meteorological approaches fail to identify which events lead to the largest
system stress, as they lack modelling and subsequent impact assesment of system interactions
such as transmission and storage dynamics. [13] uses similar stress indicators across different
years and applies clustering techniques to find representative stress situations and stochastic op-
timization framework to derive robust energy system capacity layouts. [14] analyses how many
weather years are necessary to obtain robust energy system design for USA, and concludes that
15 (if dispatchable generation is included) to 40 (if only wind, solar and energy storage are
allowed) years of weather data are required. [15] uses 60 weather years within a sector cou-
pled European energy system where single weather year is used to design a capacity layout and
then stress tested across all the other weather years. They show when using one of the extreme
weather years to design the capacity layout, it is able to achieve robust system and the total devi-
ation of system cost across all such systems are within 10 %. [16] compare capacity investment
for Belgium energy system by comparing a scenario where energy drought events are included
vs where they are not included and show that when these events are considered in capacity in-
vestment model, it leads to additional investments in hydrogen plants or small modular reactors
                                                  2
and increase the total annual cost by 14-23 % compared to not considering them. [17] in UK
case study use a cross-year two-stage modelling strategy to first find capacity layouts based on
single weather years and then expose them in the second stage by only allowing investments in
Open-cycle gas turbines (OCGT), electric boilers, batteries and hydrogen storage to make sure
the system is robust to 10 years of weather variability.
    In contrast to the system-level perspective outlined above, wind project developers approach
the representation of weather variability from a somewhat different starting point. The pre-
construction wind resource and energy yield assessment (WRA/EYA) workflow, progressively
codified by the IEC 61400-15 series with Part 15-1 on site-suitability input conditions published
in 2025 [18], and Part 15-2 on energy yield still in development and reviewed in e.g. [19], is
explicitly built around the long-term wind climate the turbines will experience over their 20-30
year operational lifetime. A typical workflow combines one to two years of on-site measure-
ments (met mast, LiDAR, or SoDAR) with a long-term reference dataset, often a reanalysis
product such as ERA5 or MERRA-2, or a nearby long-running measurement through Measure-
Correlate-Predict (MCP) techniques [20]. The short on-site record is thereby extrapolated, under
an assumption of climate stationarity, to a multi-decadal climatology taken as representative of
the project lifetime. The resulting wind distribution is propagated through microscale flow and
wake models and turbine power curves to produce annual energy production estimates which in
turn drive the financing of the project. Wind project developers thus by default use long-term
weather distributions to remove biases a single weather year may cause when planning wind
investments. This is in contrast to how the same wind generation technologies are represented
in large-scale energy system models. Whereas individual wind power plant investments are, by
construction, planned against a 20–30-year wind climate, the aggregate fleet of those investments
is often represented in capacity expansion planning using a single historical weather year. And
even if multiple weather years are used, the investment optimisation is carried out year-by-year,
not considering the long-term weather distribution. This asymmetry motivates a more systematic
treatment of long-term weather variability in the baseline investment stage, which is the focus of
this paper.
    In an ideal setting, investments would be co-optimized jointly over all 40 weather years
to ensure that the resource availability from VRE is unbiased and system adequacy is ensured
across all the years. However, due to computational complexity it is rarely possible, especially in
large scale systems with multiple sectoral representation. While previous studies have adopted
strategies to get adequate systems via using extreme single weather year to design the system and
test robustness ex-post [15], use single weather year in first stage and use different weather years
in the second stage to add additional required investments [17] or via iterative methods [21], [22],
[23], they still use single weather years as their baseline to start with. These approaches help in
getting an robust system but the baseline system on which they iterate still is represented by a
single weather year data and can introduce biases in weather-driven investments.

1.2. Contribution to the literature
    Building on the research gaps identified above, we propose a novel framework for capac-
ity expansion planning that departs from the conventional single-weather-year approach and in-
cludes information of long-term distributions in baseline investments.
    The first contribution is that we showcase significant biases in terms of anomalies that could
arise for different weather dependent generation and demand if a single weather is chosen for the
baseline investments. As argued in the previous subsection, basing weather-driven investments

                                                 3
on single weather years is not aligned with how VRE investments are planned by developers,
potentially causing unrealistic VRE investment results if a single weather year is used.
    The second contribution is that we showcase and apply a time-series scaling approach that in-
corporates the long-term distribution of weather conditions. Rather than optimizing investments
based on a single weather year, we construct scaled input time series that represent long-term
distributions considering 39 weather years, thereby enabling the baseline system to reflect unbi-
ased generation distributions across technologies and regions, better aligning VRE investments
in large-scale energy system analysis to how developers plan them. Additional advantage is that
the method is computationally efficient. To the best of our knowledge, this approach has not
been applied in previous long term planning studies of continental scale sector coupled energy
systems considering multi-decade weather data.
    To ensure robustness, we adopt a two-stage methodology where we consider the baseline
system as exogenous and allow additional investments optimized for extreme weather years (as
identified from the stress-test of the baseline system) in order to achieve adequacy. This is similar
to [17] but our approach differs because the first stage considers multiple weather years instead
of multiple single year optimization. Moreover, we apply the two-stage optimization to a larger
system (pan-European vs UK and its surrounding countries) and increase the temporal scope of
weather data (multi-decade vs 10 years).
    Finally, We demonstrate the proposed approach on a pan-European, sector-coupled energy
system using 39 years of weather data. We show that compared to energy system designs based
on single extreme weather years, it achieves comparable adequacy, stability in VRE investments
in all second-stage results and relies of different flexibility providing technologies for adequacy.
We argue it provides more realistic (i.e., better aligned to how developers operate) VRE invest-
ments.

2. Methodology

2.1. Weather dependent input data
    This section describes how different weather dependent components are modeled in the over-
all energy system model. Weather dependent input time series is generated for Solar-PV, Wind
and Hydro generation, Coefficient of performance (COP) of heat-pumps and electricity and heat
demand. Reanalysis data from ERA5 [24] is utilized as primary source for hourly resolution
weather data which is taken as input for all the different component models mentioned above.
Having a consistent weather input data ensures that the correlations between different weather
variables are maintained. The rest of the section describes how each of the weather-dependent
components of the energy system is modeled.




                                                 4
2.1.1. VRE modelling
    Wind and solar generation time series are simualated using the CorRES tool [25],[26]. The
methodology to create standardized generation follows [27], consisting of micro-scale adjust-
ment via Global Wind Atlas version 2 (GWA2) and surrogate power-curve model to account for
intra-farm wake losses.
    Multiple wind and solar PV generation technologies are modelled and differentiated for mul-
tiple resource grades as implemented in [3], [28]. The detailed description and the data-set used
are available at [29].

2.1.2. Temperature dependent demand and heat pump COP modelling
    T2m variable from ERA5 is used as surface level temperatures. This is used to derive profiles
for the heat and electricity demand and heat-pump COPs. Heat demand profiles are modelled
using degree days defination and later scaled to yearly level based on annual energy demand
levels as defined in the current data scenario for Balmorel for each regions. Air-to-Water and
Air-to-Air heat pumps COP are modelled based based on [30] and electricity demand is modelled
based on [31].

2.1.3. Hydro inflow modelling
    The hydropower inflow is estimated using reanalysis river discharge data from GloFAS-
ERA5 Version 4.0 [32] against ENTSO-E historical data [33], including both reservoir and run-
of-river inflows. First, historical reservoir inflow from 2015 to 2025 is derived from weekly
water storage levels and reservoir generation data from ENTSO-E, while the run-of-river inflow
is obtained directly from historical run-of-river generation data. Next, the GloFAS river dis-
charge data in all weather years are retrieved by the hydropower plants’ coordinates provided by
OpenStreetMap [34]. Finally, a hybrid regression model combining Random Forest and Ridge
Regression is trained on historical data to estimate inflow across all weather years. The estimated
inflow is validated using leave-one-year-out cross-validation within the observed years, as well
as long-term validation across all weather years, as illustrated in Figure A.16 and Figure A.17.
Detailed information and modelled data can be found in [35].

2.2. Scaling of time series
    The time series scaling uses the 39-year weather data to represent the long-term distributions
of weather-dependent variables. Due to computational complexity, only less than 1 year of data
can be used in the Balmorel capacity expansion modelling [28], [36]. The following describes
how the long-term distribution information is integrated into this limited time step selection. The

capacity optimization model (discussed later in Section 2.3.1) performs optimization with a one-
year foresight using reduced representative time series. A subset of time-series data from a single
weather year is selected for the optimization and subsequently scaled to represent the 39 weather
years using the following method.
    The weather-dependent generation or demand (onshore wind, solar PV, heat demand, etc.) in
region r at time t is defined as pr,t , with the vector considering all the R regions being

days and weeks, selected to be representative of full year as discussed earlier.
     The statistical properties of pt, f ull and pt,short are different, especially if the T start to T end
covers a significantly shorter time range than 1 to T. However, the data transformation shown

                                       p∗r,t,short = Fr,−1f ull (Fr,short (pr,t,short ))                           (2)
      where Fr,short is the Cumulative distribution function (CDF) of pr,t,short and Fr, f ull is the CDF

The resulting vector p∗t,short covers the time range T start to T end but transformed so that all the

    Equation 2 makes the marginal distributions of pt,short same as that of pt, f ull ; for example, for
VRE generation this ensures that the pt,short represents the long-term annual yields and generation

context of energy system planning models while maintaining the essential temporal patterns of
the data.

2.3. Modelling Chain
2.3.1. Balmorel Energy System Modelling framework
    This study uses the Balmorel energy-system modelling framework [37], a widely applied
bottom-up, partial-equilibrium optimization model for European energy analyses (e.g., [38],[28],
[39],[40], [4]). Balmorel minimizes total system costs subject to energy-balance, technological,
and policy constraints, and can represent multiple sectors including electricity, heating, industry,
and transport. The model configuration employed here follows the versions used in [38], [4].
The source code for model and the input data for this analysis are publicly available in [41],[42].
We use Balmorel in two complementary modes: capacity optimization to determine cost-optimal
investments and dispatch optimization to evaluate hourly operation and adequacy for the resulting
capacity mix.
    The capacity optimization is formulated as a greenfield 2050 investment problem. Retire-
ments of the existing asset stock are incorporated exogenously, and the model endogenously
selects the least-cost set of new capacities needed to satisfy 2050 demand subject to technical
and policy constraints. The objective function minimizes total system costs, including annual-
ized investment costs and operating costs. Temporal variability is represented by a reduced set
of selected days with 3-hour resolution (see Section 3 ).
    In the dispatch optimization model, capacities obtained from the capacity optimization are
treated as exogenous, with no additional capacity investment allowed. The model is solved
for one weather year at a time and determines the cost-minimal operation of all technologies
at hourly resolution. The dispatch optimization modelling follows [43] and consists of three
sequential steps. First, a full-year optimization with perfect foresight is conducted at 4-hour
resolution to determine long-term storage levels and planned maintenance schedules. Second, a
stochastic outage model introduces unplanned forced outages. Third, a rolling-horizon optimiza-
tion schedules hourly operation over the year, solving one day at a time.
    To ensure feasibility in hours where the installed capacity portfolio cannot meet demand,
generic backup technologies are introduced across sectors. These backup options are gas-based
and include a generic gas generator in the electricity sector, a generic gas boiler in the heat
sector, and a generic electrolyzer or bio-methane hydrogen generator in the hydrogen sector.

                                                   8
Backup technologies are assumed to be available with effectively unconstrained capacity. Their
operating costs are not parameterized as a value of lost load (VoLL). Instead, they are inherently
expensive due to natural-gas fuel costs and associated CO2 taxation. In this study, activation of
backup technologies is interpreted as a proxy for inadequacy, indicating that the baseline capacity
portfolio and its operational flexibility are insufficient to meet demand under the given conditions.
Backup activation is recorded at hourly resolution, defined per region for electricity and hydrogen
and per heat area for the heat sector, consistent with the respective energy-balance formulations.
Based on this proxy, we compute a set of adequacy metrics, which are subsequently used to
analyze the adequacy of the baseline capacity portfolio across weather years. These metrics are

2.3.2. Two-stage modelling framework
    The overall modelling chain is illustrated in Figure 3. A weather-to-energy module generates
hourly input datasets for all weather-dependent components (as described in Section 2.1) for the
period 1982–2020.
    In the first stage, capacity optimization determines a baseline set of technology capacities.
For each weather year, a set of representative time-steps (described in section 3) is selected
and then scaled following the method described in Section 2.2. This is applied to all weather
dependent time series inputs. This gives 39 systems with different capacity mix. Each of the
system is evaluated based on system cost and the median cost system is selected as the baseline
system.
    In the second stage, the baseline capacities are treated as fixed, and additional investments
are permitted. This optimization is conducted separately for different identified extreme weather
year, yielding an augmented system that addresses conditions under which the initial capacity
mix fails to ensure adequacy. During the second stage investment, individual weather years are
not scaled to match the long term distribution.
    We compare the proposed two-stage model with the a single-weather-year based model where
the capacity are optimized for individual weather years without any scaling. The results from
these are also used to identify extreme weather years which are used for second-stage capacity
optimization. Capacity mix from both the models are then stress tested with 39 years of dispatch
optmization model and their cost and adequacy are evaluated.

4.1. Impact of weather years on Levelized cost of Electricity (LCOE)
    In this section, we show the impact of time-series scaling. Figure 5 shows levelized cost of
electricity (LCOE) for a chosen onshore wind technology and resource grade (described in Sec-
tion 2.1.1 for different regions when the long-term weather is considered (refereed in the figure
as climatology) and when percentage anomaly when individual weather years are considered.
It can be seen that when individual weather years are considered for investment planning, the
LCOE can vary more than 20 % for some of the regions. Moreover, even if a weather year is
chosen which results on average least deviation to LCOE calculated based on long-term weather
conditions (eg. 2012 and 1999), still some of the regions can exhibit large deviations. For ex-
ample, in the weather year 2012 (which on average resulted into 0.3 % anomaly across regions),
Portugal had +7 % more and Denmark had 4 to 5 % less LCOE for the same technology. For the
year 1999 which on average had -0.7 % anomaly across all regions, some regions had -8 to -11 %
anomaly and multiple other regions had more than + 6 % anomaly. This shows how investment
signals can be distorted for some of the technologies in some of the regions when individual
weather years are considered. Figure A.14 and A.13 show similar analysis for offshore wind and
solar-PV technologies.

4.1.1. Determination of the Baseline system and extreme weather years
    This section evaluates how the time-series scaling procedure affects total system cost. The
results are then used to define the baseline system and select extreme weather years, which are
subsequently used to guide 2nd-stage adequacy-driven investments.
    Figure 6, compares total system costs across weather years using raw weather-year inputs
and scaled time series. Costs vary substantially more under raw inputs, and the set of most
expensive years differs once scaling is applied. Modest variation remains after scaling, as the
procedure harmonizes marginal distributions but does not standardize the joint spatiotemporal
dependence across weather-driven supply and demand; consequently, different realizations with
the same marginals can still yield different system outcomes. We select the median-cost scaled
case (1995) as the baseline, interpreted as a pragmatic average-cost realization under the scaled
distribution. Weather year 1985, 2010, 1987 and 1996 (the four weather years which had the
highest system cost when raw weather year data is used) are selected as extreme years which are
used to guide targeted second-stage investments for the two-stage model.

years. We focus on inadequacy faced in electricity balance as there are well-defined reliability

4.3. Comparison of two-stage optimization approach vs single-weather-year approach
    In this section we compare capacity mix and total cost of different models. Namely, two-stage
models where the baseline system is further reinforced in the 2nd stage based on identified ex-
treme weather years, utilizing raw weather data (i.e., the data are not scaled). They are compared
with single-weather-year models for the same weather years (using raw weather data).
    Figure 10 shows the capacity mix of the baseline system (for reference), two-stage model
where the baseline system is reinforced with additional capacity by optimizing over selected

two-stage models. Conversely, the two-stage models make considerably larger investments in
long-duration hydrogen storage and fuel cells. A similar tendency is observed for electricity
transmission, with higher transmission investments occurring under the two-stage approach.

5. Discussion

5.1. Discussion of Results
    A central contribution of this work is the argument that bulk capacity-expansion decisions
should be anchored in a system design reflecting the long-term weather distribution, rather than
depending on single weather years. The paper demonstrates that single-weather-year based plan-
ning can introduce biases in weather-dependent resources (upto 20 % change in LCOE for on-
shore wind technologies, 15 % for solar and 25 % for offshore wind technologies). To reduce
these biases while remaining computationally tractable, the proposed scaling method aligns the
marginal distributions of selected time steps for capacity optimization with the long-term dis-
tributions derived from the full multi-decade weather data. This supports the broader objective
of creating a baseline investment signal that is more consistent with how VRE investments are
evaluated in practice, i.e., against long-term climatology rather than a single-weather-year real-
ization.
    We also show that as future energy systems become more and more weather dependent,
weather variability arises as an important driver of inadequacy, as shown in Figures 8 and 9,
where multiple regions have LOLE above 3 hrs/year. This showcases the importance of stress
testing any proposed capacity-mix for future VRE dominated energy system across multiple
decades of weather data.
    Finally, We compare the outcomes of the proposed two-stage approach where the inade-
quate baseline system is further reinforced in the second stage by further optimizing the system
over some of the identified extreme weather years to directly optimizing over the same extreme
weather years (single-weather-year based models). As shown in Figure 10, we find that after
the baseline system is determined, additional investments for ensuring adequacy do not rely on
VRE technologies. Whereas, single-weather-year based models can results in varying amount of
VRE investment. We claim that while those VRE invesment levels may be optimal for a specific
weather year, they do not reflect the way developers are actually planning VRE investments and
thus are not reflective of expected VRE investments towards 2050. In terms of flexibility provid-
ing technologies, the proposed two-stage model relies more of fuell cells and combines it with
                                                       18
H2 storage and electricity transmission whereas the single-weather-year based models generally
see less electrified heating and more CHPs.

5.2. Limitations and future work
    Although the proposed scaling method adjusts the marginal distributions to reflect the long-
term distributions, the differences in investment outcomes in Figure 6 suggest that the composi-
tion of the selected representative time steps can still influence least-cost investment outcomes.
This remaining sensitivity is potentially because the scaling approach does not enforce the joint
spatio-temporal dependence structure of weather-driven variables (autocorrelations, spatial cor-
relations, and cross-variable relationships between wind/solar/hydro/demand). As shown in Fig-
ure 6, variation in system cost remains even after applying the scaling procedure. These findings
motivate a clear methodological direction: future “representative baseline” construction should
aim to preserve not only marginal distributions but also time-series dependence and spatial cor-
relations. Developing such representative baselines could yield a more stable representation of
long-term climate behavior for bulk investment decisions, while still enabling computationally
feasible planning models.
    The presented modelling framework also introduces certain other limitation. First, the op-
erational adequacy assessment includes a full-year optimization step with perfect foresight to
determine long-term storage levels and maintenance scheduling, whereas in practice, the oper-
ational bids are derived from estimated water value. Even the daily rolling-horizon operations
are dispatched based on real available power and demand whereas these are often based on
short-to-medium term forecast. This may understate operational difficulty when dealing with
forecast uncertainty due to large amount of weather dependency in the system. Future work
could address this limitation by explicitly accounting for forecast errors or incorporating ade-
quate reserve margins in the dispatch optimization, thereby enabling the framework to better
capture the operational challenges associated with forecast-dependent system dispatch. Second,
weather-dependent components are modelled based on historical relationships and reanalysis-
driven inputs; climate change may alter temperature distributions, wind regimes, hydro inflows.
Although the proposed approach allows this; but the input data would need to be based on cli-
mate change projections data instead of reanalysis. Third, while forced outages are represented
through a stochastic outage model, outage probabilities may themselves be weather-dependent
in reality (e.g., floods, heatwaves, storms etc.)[44].
    Finally, although this study primarily focused on adequacy in the electricity sector, notable
inadequacy was also observed in the heat and hydrogen balances. A LOLE threshold of 3 h/year
across European regions as suggested in [23] as a reliability standard for the power sector, and
this benchmark was therefore adopted in the present analysis for the power sector. In contrast,
comparable reliability standards for the heat and hydrogen sectors are not yet well established,
and consequently these carriers were not included in the main adequacy assessment. Moreover,
assumptions related to the cost of backup technologies or the value of lost load (VOLL) for heat
and hydrogen may shift adequacy challenges between sectors and thereby influence the overall
results. Figure A.15 presents the regional LOLE and EENS values for both heat and hydrogen
across the model variants. For the heat sector, the two-stage model generally performs better
than the single-weather-year based model, whereas the opposite trend is observed for hydrogen.
Nevertheless, for both carriers, EENS remains below approximately 5–10 TWh/year in most re-
gions, which is relatively small compared with the corresponding annual energy demand. Future
research should focus on defining reliability standards for non-electric energy carriers and on im-

                                                19
proving the characterization of sector-specific VOLL values, so that adequacy in sector-coupled
energy systems can be assessed in a more consistent and rigorous manner.


6. Conclusion

     This study demonstrates that long-term energy system planning for highly weather-dependent,
sector-coupled systems cannot rely on a single weather year without risking distorted investment
signals. In the pan-European 2050 case study, we show that inter-annual weather variability af-
fects LCOE of VRE technologies upto 20 % when relying on single weather years compared to
when long-term weather conditions are incorporated. Using multiple weather years is also how
VRE developers assesses individual projects’ economic viability and hence decision for invest-
ments. To address this challenge, this paper applies a computationally tractable scaling approach
that embeds long-term weather distributions into the baseline capacity expansion problem. This
allows the planning model to better reflect the climatological conditions under which weather-
dependent assets are actually valued, while avoiding the full computational burden of explicit
multi-decadal optimization.
     The results show that optimizing using a single weather year leads to substantial variation
in VRE investments. Technologies or regions may appear more attractive due to year-specific
weather conditions rather than their performance under long-term climatological conditions,
thereby distorting investment signals. In contrast, when the baseline system is optimized us-
ing long-term weather distributions, VRE investment levels remain stable even when extreme
weather years are considered in the second-stage. We argue that this outcome is more consistent
with real-world investment decision-making.
     The results indicate that maintaining adequacy under extreme conditions is not obtained by
additional VRE capacity. Instead, system reinforcement is achieved primarily through invest-
ments in flexibility resources, including long-duration hydrogen storage, fuel cells and electric-
ity transmission. Compared with the single-weather-year based model, the two-stage model also
results in somewhat lower CHP investments and higher electrification of the heating sector.
     When using the proposed framework, the resulting system remains adequate from electrical
power system perspective when using different 2nd stage extreme weather years, whereas in the
case of single-weather-year based models, only one of the extreme years achieved the same level
of adequacy.
     Overall, the study shows that incorporating long-term weather information in the baseline
stage can provide an unbiased and realistic starting point for planning, while targeted reinforce-
ments remain necessary to ensure robustness under extreme conditions.
